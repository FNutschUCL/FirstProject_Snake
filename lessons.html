Why some requests work better
Lesson · 4 min read
Video: What the AI actually sees 2 min

You've now asked for a few things and seen some work better than others. That isn't luck. It comes down to one idea:

The assistant can read your project, but it cannot read your mind.

It knows every file you have. It does not know what you pictured, what you'd consider "better", or which of five possible meanings you meant.

So the difference between a request that works and one that disappoints is usually how much context you gave it — not how technical your words were.

Compare these two:

Thin make it better The assistant has to guess what "better" means. You'll get something — just probably not what you pictured.
Rich make the game harder — speed it up as the snake grows, and make the food appear further from the snake's head Goal, plus two concrete ways to get there. Almost no room to misread you.

You don't need technical words. "Make it feel like an old arcade machine" is a rich request. "Improve the UI" is a thin one, despite sounding more expert.

7
What the assistant actually is
Lesson · 5 min read
Video: Why it sounds certain even when it's wrong 3 min

You've been working with it for a while now. It's worth knowing what it is, because that explains almost everything it does — the good and the annoying.

It predicts what text should come next. It does not look things up, and it does not know things.

That single fact has consequences you'll feel every day:

  • It sounds equally confident when right and when wrong. Confidence is part of the writing style it learned, not a signal about accuracy.
  • It can invent things that look plausible. A function that doesn't exist, a setting that was never there. This is normal and expected, not a malfunction.
  • It forgets. Close the conversation and start a new one, and it knows nothing about what you did before — except what it can read in your files.
  • It reads your project. That's why it's useful here. It's not guessing about your game; it can actually open the file.

None of this makes it unreliable. It makes it a very fast collaborator who is sometimes wrong and never says so — which is exactly why the next lesson is about checking.

8
Checking the work
Lesson · 6 min · the important one

This is the lesson that separates people who use AI from people who work with it.

The temptation is obvious: it produced something, it sounded sure, the change appeared. Accept and move on. Do that often enough and you end up with a project you don't understand and can't fix.

You don't need to read the code. You do need to check the result.

Three habits, in order of how much they're worth:

  • Run it. Every single time. Reload the game and look. This catches most problems in five seconds.
  • Try to break it. Asked for a pause button? Press it twice. Press it while the snake is dying. Odd cases are where things fall over.
  • Ask it to explain itself. "What exactly did you change and why?" If the explanation doesn't match what you asked for, something drifted.

9
When it breaks
Lesson · 4 min read

At some point your game will go blank, freeze, or start behaving like a completely different game. This is not a sign that you're doing badly. It happens to everyone who builds anything, every day.

Breaking things is not failure. It's most of the job.

What matters is that you have a way back. You have three:

  • Say what you see. Not "it's broken" — rather "the screen is blank after I clicked pause". The assistant can't see your screen, so what you observed is the single most useful thing you can give it.
  • Ask it to undo. "That didn't work, please put it back the way it was." It knows what it just changed.
  • Start the conversation fresh. If it's gone in circles for several attempts, a new conversation often works better than a fourth attempt in the old one.

One thing worth knowing: your project is stored with its full history. Even a thoroughly broken version can be returned to how it was this morning. Nothing you do here is permanent.

+
Optional: take it home

This one is genuinely optional. You've finished the course either way, and skipping it costs you nothing.

Everything so far has run in your browser, on GitHub's computers. That was deliberate — it's why you never had to install anything. But it has limits: the workspace is eventually cleared away if you don't use it, and you're always one internet connection away from your own work.

If you'd like the same setup on your own machine, permanently, it's four steps:

  • Install Visual Studio Code — the same editor you've been using, from code.visualstudio.com
  • Install the Cline extension inside it, and sign in as you did here
  • Install Git, which is how you copy a project down to your computer
  • Ask the assistant: "walk me through cloning this project to my own computer, step by step, assuming I know nothing"

Why bother: your projects stay yours and stay put, everything runs faster, and you can work offline. Why not to bother right now: it's fiddly, and none of it makes you better at the actual skill you came for.
